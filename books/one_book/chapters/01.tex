\chapter{What does an economy keep alive?}\label{ch:motivation}

An economy makes promises about the future. A wage promises purchasing power; a maintenance plan promises a working bridge; a water service promises something usable when a household opens its tap. These promises depend on different records and different kinds of evidence. This book begins by asking how they can remain connected, then develops a precise account of the physical changes on which continuing service depends.

\section{Why people made money}
No household can produce everything it needs, and an exchange is difficult when each person must want exactly what the other can offer at that moment. Communities have found ways to record obligations, compare contributions and carry claims across time. Historical records include accounts on clay tablets, measured commodities, weighed metal and coins. Money developed through several forms and institutions; its history is not one universal passage from simple barter to coinage.\cite{intro-money-history}

A shared monetary unit makes a remarkable form of cooperation possible. A carpenter can receive payment without taking whatever food the customer happens to have. A clinic can order supplies from a distant manufacturer. An obligation can be recorded today and discharged later. Trust, enforceable agreements and functioning institutions support these arrangements. Money's familiar roles as a unit of account, means of exchange and store of value help cooperation extend beyond the immediate encounter.\cite{intro-money-functions}

The achievement is real. It also has a defined scope. Suppose the clinic pays its electricity bill and orders medicine, but a failed pipe leaves its treatment room without water. The payments and orders may all be correctly recorded. They do not make the missing service available. That service depends on reservoirs, treatment, pipes, electricity, stocks already in transit and people able to maintain the chain.

The reverse situation is possible too. Someone repairs the pipe without making a sale, and usable service returns. The repair has changed the town even if no revenue records its benefit. A financial account and a physical account can therefore describe the same day accurately while reporting different things.

\section{A measure can be excellent at the wrong question}
A monetary sum is useful partly because it can travel across very different transactions. It need not describe every property of the object purchased. Yet two products with the same sale price can use different materials, leave different wastes and have different consequences for future service. Equal receipts do not imply equal physical changes.

This is not a claim that profitable activity is necessarily damaging. Maintenance can be profitable, and a loss-making project can waste resources. The more precise point is that no accounting identity makes private financial return equal the improvement of a declared physical condition. Connecting the two requires knowledge and a rule.

Existing societies already make such connections through contracts, public services, standards, taxes, regulation and shared obligations. A water-quality standard identifies a condition a supplier must respect; a maintenance contract makes a future service part of a present agreement. Their effectiveness depends on specifying the condition, observing it and responding when the promise fails. The financial number does not perform those tasks by itself.

The EBU proposal begins at this measurement problem. It asks what physical state an action starts from, what complete change it produces, and how that change compares with a declared potential. The potential is a mathematical description of a specified concern. It is not a claim that one number can contain every purpose of human life. The rest of the book will make both the power and the boundary of that account explicit.

\section{Want, need and the power to pay}
A person's ability to make an offer is not the same as the urgency of their need. A buyer may be able to pay for a second convenience while another person cannot afford a first safe water supply. Both can be represented in a market, but their offers do not automatically rank the consequences of going without.

The difficulty becomes sharper when those affected cannot participate in the transaction. A future child cannot bid today for an intact aquifer. A downstream household may have no contract with the activity that changes its water. Institutions can represent those interests, but doing so is a deliberate achievement. It does not follow from the existence of a price.

A physical account could make some neglected consequences more inspectable. For example, a maintenance record could show the source stock used, the usable receiver stock restored and the material sent to a sink. Such a record would give a community evidence for deciding how to support essential repair. It would not decide the repairer's pay, the patient's entitlement or the community's priorities.

This distinction is also a source of efficiency. When a problem is described accurately, people can spend less effort disputing the wrong question. They can distinguish an empty reservoir from a broken route, a delivery still pending from one that never occurred, and a repaired service from a paid invoice. The possible social gain is more dependable provision. It depends on credible observations and institutions willing to use them.

\begin{intuitionbox}{The question of this book}
What does an action take from the represented world, what does it leave there, and which continuing function depends on the change? A useful physical account makes that comparison clear enough to inform a human decision without pretending to replace it.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{A world inherited rather than purchased}
Every generation receives a world it did not manufacture. Sunlight arrives, water circulates, organisms reproduce, and people build and maintain the networks through which these processes support shelter, food and care. Keeping that inheritance usable does not mean freezing every stock at its present value. A living system continually changes; continuing function depends on the right changes occurring in the right places and at the right times.

A repaired wetland, a maintained water line or unpaid care can preserve possibilities that a sales total describes poorly. Conversely, an activity can produce revenue while reducing tomorrow's options. The challenge is to make those consequences visible without claiming that every worthwhile human contribution can already be measured on one universal scale.

We shall build the argument in that order. First identify states and complete physical transitions. Then learn how sequential changes fit together and why simultaneous actions may have a joint effect that differs from their separate effects. Only after valuation is clear will we ask how a response law, memory or feedback produces an endpoint, and under which additional conditions probability and entropy describe the same comparison.

The immediate next step is closer to ordinary life: a workshop must choose what to do with a day's labour. Following that choice will show how an accurate physical account might inform an incentive while remaining distinct from the incentive itself.
