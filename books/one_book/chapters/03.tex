\chapter{Scarcity is more than an empty store}\label{ch:scarcity}

The clinic's missing water could have several causes. Treating them as one shortage would make the response wasteful: more dispatch will not repair a broken pipe, and a new pipe will not settle a right of access. This chapter explains why the represented state must distinguish resource, route, timing and entitlement before any number can describe an intervention usefully.

\section{Water behind a locked gate}
A reservoir contains water, yet the clinic receives none. Perhaps the pipe has failed. Perhaps the water is unsafe. Perhaps a delivery is still pending, or the owner has refused access. Each explanation is consistent with a positive stock total. Each calls for a different action.

Three questions help separate them. What usable resource exists within the declared boundary? Can it reach the required function in time? Who is permitted and able to receive it? Supply and demand, infrastructure and access can all contribute to water scarcity; the FAO's account likewise treats scarcity as more than a single stock figure.\cite{intro-water}

Some constraints are physical. The district cannot distribute more usable water than it can obtain, treat or safely reuse over the relevant interval. Other constraints arise from a route, a schedule or a rule. A physically full store can coexist with exclusion. Neither observation proves that all scarcity is natural or all scarcity is artificial. It tells us to identify the constraint before recommending a remedy.

For an EBU system, this implies a practical requirement: the physical description must include the coordinates that distinguish relevant alternatives. If pending water is omitted, a second order can look like a first response. If unusable water is pooled with safe water, a favourable total can conceal failed service. A smaller model is computationally cheaper only when it still preserves the distinctions needed for the decision.

\section{Competition is a choice of allocation rule}
When several people seek a constrained resource, a monetary bid is one possible way to allocate it. A queue, service priority, entitlement or negotiated share is another. An account of the physical change can inform these rules without selecting one of them.

The same distinction applies to cooperation. Shared need does not guarantee agreement, and a common resource does not guarantee that independently chosen actions will fit together. Two deliveries may draw on the same source; two repairs may support the same service. Adding their separate descriptions can miss what happens when both occur. The later coalition mathematics will make this joint comparison exact for a declared table.

There is no need to condemn every act of maximisation to see the difficulty. The decisive questions are what is being improved and which constraints remain binding. Improving private return, reducing a represented burden and protecting essential access are different objectives. An institution can choose how to combine them, but it should make that choice visible.

Better visibility can improve efficiency by directing effort toward the actual bottleneck. A verified permission problem belongs with governance; a source problem belongs with supply; a timing problem may require coordination. Calling all three a low EBU value would obscure useful information rather than organise it.

\section{Inequality changes exposure as well as income}
The World Bank's \emph{Poverty, Prosperity, and Planet Report 2024} estimated that about 700 million people lived in extreme poverty under its then-used \$2.15-a-day line. It described stalled progress and the combined challenge of poverty, unequal opportunity and climate risk. The figure is a dated estimate under a named monetary definition, not a count of every kind of deprivation.\cite{intro-worldbank}

The WHO's 2025 report on social determinants of health equity examines how conditions of birth, work, housing and access to power, money and resources shape health. Its comparisons show why a national average cannot represent everyone's circumstances.\cite{intro-health} A physical model has an analogous responsibility: aggregation must not erase the differences relevant to the claim being made.

A district can have sufficient water in total while one clinic has none. A road may work for people with vehicles but provide no practical route for others. A medicine can exist while being unavailable at the time a patient needs it. Representing these differences does not solve distribution. It allows a distributional claim to be checked against something more informative than an average.

The possible benefit is a more accountable discussion of service. People could challenge a reassuring total with evidence about an omitted place, time or condition. Such an account would need privacy, accessible explanation and a way to correct errors; measurement alone does not guarantee that those affected will be heard.

\section{Resources, grievance and conflict}
Resource control and exclusion can be part of a conflict, but they do not provide a universal explanation of violence. The World Bank identifies economic exclusion among contributors to fragility alongside governance and other causes.\cite{intro-conflict} The IPCC finds conditional links between climate variability, extremes and conflict risk, while giving greater weight to major social and political drivers.\cite{intro-ipcc-conflict}

An EBU account should respect that complexity. Showing that two towns share one river does not settle their rights or motives. It can help prevent a negotiation from proceeding as though each town had the full flow independently available. A common physical description supports the discussion; it does not decide what a just agreement is.

The later distinction between a joint outcome and an allocation of responsibility has the same purpose. Mathematical decomposition can identify a dependence in a declared comparison. It cannot infer consent, ownership or blame from that dependence alone.

\begin{intuitionbox}{Find the failure before choosing the remedy}
Too little usable resource, no workable route in time, and no effective right to receive it are different failures. A useful account keeps them distinguishable so that an intervention addresses the problem actually present.
\end{intuitionbox}
