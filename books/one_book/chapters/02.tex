\chapter{What money rewards, and what it cannot see}\label{ch:money}

\section{The decision at the workshop}
A workshop can spend a day repairing a town's water line or use that day to finish an order for a customer who will pay immediately. Its workers need wages; its tools and electricity cost money; it cannot simply ignore its accounts. The paid order is therefore a serious claim on its time. The pipe is also a serious claim, because households and a clinic depend on it. The difficulty is not that one claim is real and the other imaginary. It is that the two arrive in different languages.

In a familiar business calculation, an activity is attractive when expected revenue exceeds the private costs borne by the decision-maker. Written schematically,
\begin{equation}
 \text{expected private return}
 =\text{expected receipts}-\text{private expenditure}.
\end{equation}
This is not a complete description of every person's motivation. People act from duty, affection, law, fear and curiosity as well as financial return. But when an institution must remain solvent, the sign of this difference has practical force. A repair that protects many homes can be difficult to undertake if the workshop cannot pay for it; an activity with a buyer can proceed even if some physical costs fall elsewhere.

Prices convey important information about willingness and ability to pay, costs, scarcity under existing rights and expectations. They are not a general sensor of the future condition of a catchment, a patient's health or the integrity of a bridge. The distinction is well known in economics as an \emph{externality}: an action affects others in ways its market price does not include. The IMF's account of externalities uses pollution as a standard example, and its energy-subsidy work notes that retail fuel prices often do not cover environmental costs.\cite{intro-externality,intro-imf-fossil} Such an omitted effect does not become unreal because it is absent from a receipt.

\section{A dollar is a claim, not a physical forecast}
A bank deposit, a wage and a sale are parts of a monetary system that allocates claims and makes exchange possible. In modern banking, deposits can also be created through lending; the Bank of England describes the process and its constraints in detail.\cite{intro-boe} None of these facts implies that money is unreal or that every loan requires growth forever. The relevant observation is simpler: a unit of currency is not stamped with the amount of clean water, usable soil, climatic stability or care that will exist when it is spent.

The workshop may receive the same amount for two orders. One consumes scarce spare parts and shortens the life of a pump; another repairs the pump. The money entering its account is equal, while the future physical options differ. A good set of prices, contracts and regulations can narrow that gap. Yet the physical consequence has to be known or estimated before it can be brought into a rule. One cannot repair a missing measurement by assuming that the monetary number already contains it.

The problem reaches across generations. Future people cannot sign today's contracts. Some long-lived damage will be carried by them even though no invoice names them. Present institutions can and do act on their behalf, but the incentive is an explicit political and ethical achievement, not a property of a banknote.

\section{Demand, desire and indispensable service}
A demand curve records offers under a distribution of purchasing power. It does not rank claims by biological urgency. A household can want an additional convenience while another lacks its first safe supply. If the first can pay and the second cannot, the former may have greater market demand. This does not mean producers are indifferent to suffering. It means that an economy organised around sale needs additional arrangements if indispensable functions are to remain available independently of the buyer's wealth.

Public water systems, health provision, subsidies, insurance and regulation are examples of those additional arrangements. Their existence makes a crude attack on money misleading. They also reveal the underlying issue: societies repeatedly have to connect financial decisions to outcomes that financial transactions alone do not guarantee. How well that connection works depends on what is measured, who can act and whose interests count.

One response is to adjust prices until every physical effect is priced. That may be useful for some effects, especially where they can be measured, attributed and governed. It is harder where a loss is irreversible, where a service has no buyer, or where a single price hides unequal exposure. We should therefore treat money as a powerful coordination instrument with a defined job, not as a universal account of all the conditions that make life possible.

\section{Where an incentive points}
Imagine that a supplier can earn ten monetary units by selling water to a distant buyer and six by supplying a local clinic. If the accounts omit the clinic's health consequences, the larger sale looks better in the supplier's private calculation. If a rule pays for protecting access, the comparison changes. If a drought makes export physically dangerous, even a high offered price cannot create water. The three statements refer to different objects: private return, institutional incentive and physical possibility.

In this book we will eventually ask whether a physical account can make one of those objects more visible. It is not a promise that a new number should replace every price. It is a demand that an action's represented physical consequence be available for scrutiny when incentives are designed. A number that can be audited can influence a rule; a rule can influence a choice; repeated choices can influence a future. Each link matters, and none should be silently supplied by the previous one.

\begin{workedbox}{Two ledgers for one decision}
Suppose a repair costs the workshop eight units of money and earns only five today. Its private return is $-3$. If the repair prevents a measured loss of water, that physical fact does not turn $-3$ into $+3$. It gives a community a reason to design a payment, obligation or public service that makes the repair possible. The two accounts inform one decision without being equated.
\end{workedbox}

\section{The next difficulty: who can reach what exists?}
Even perfect information about a product's price would not tell us whether everyone who needs it can obtain it. Goods can be present and still be inaccessible because of location, quality, rights, wealth or conflict. The next chapter examines this gap. It is where scarcity stops being a single total and becomes a question about people, routes and power.
